---
title: Polytown
description: A new idle town builder — claim a plot on the endless grid, house your townsfolk, turn wheat into luxuries, and sell it all at the town hall or to other mayors.
date: 2026-09-06
---

- **Your plot** — every mayor starts with a free 8×8 plot. Houses bring residents, residents staff industry, and every industry tile makes the town a little less happy. Happiness sets how fast the whole town ticks, so parks and bread are not decoration.
- **Six tiers of goods** — wheat, wood and stone feed mills, sawmills and kilns; flour and planks become bread and tools; tools open the iron mine, steel, machines and finally luxuries. Each tier is gated on running the tier before it, and higher tiers cost the goods of the lower ones, not just coins.
- **Levels** — every building upgrades to level 20. Output, workers and effects scale with level; cost and build time climb with it.
- **Two markets** — the town hall always buys at a floor price and sells at a ceiling, so you can never be stuck with stock. Between the two sits a player order book per resource: list offers, fill crossing ones instantly at the resting price, watch the trades roll in live.
- **Land office** — more plots cost more and open more slowly: ten minutes for the second, tripling after that, up to twelve.
- **Milestones** — twenty one-time rewards that double as the guide from first house to first emporium.
- **Layout matters** — a house beside a park is a happier house; a house beside a quarry is not.
- **Welcome back** — the town keeps producing for up to eight hours while you are away, and tells you what it made when you return.
- **Rush** — one gem per five minutes left on any build or upgrade.
- **Mayors** — a leaderboard ranked by what each town is built to earn per day.
- **Townsfolk needs** — residents eat grain and bread every tick, and once the town grows they want tools and luxuries too. Every need you supply raises happiness; a town with no food at all starves and slows down. The goods are really consumed, so a big town has to keep producing or buy from other mayors.
- **Radius, not just adjacency** — a park cheers every house within two tiles, industry sours the ones right beside it, and the reach is drawn on the ground while you place.
- **Everything costs goods** — upgrades take coins and resources at every level, so your own chains feed your growth and the market has real demand.
- **Buying is player-only** — the town hall still buys anything at a floor price so you can always cash out, but the only way to buy is another mayor's offer.
- **Roads** — every building's front door (the white arrow while placing) must touch a road, and roads start at the edge of your land. A building cut off from the road stops working and wears a big red **!** until you reconnect or move it. Buildings can be moved for free. Townsfolk stroll the streets.
- **Moods** — happiness is a ladder: Miserable, Uneasy, Content, Happy, Thriving. Each step has visible perks (production, build time, storage), and the happiness bar shows what the town can reach with what it can make right now.
- **Priced by payback** — the first of every building pays for itself in about a week at floor prices; every extra copy costs more, houses most of all. Land goes 50k, 225k, 1M, 4.5M…
- **Tiers take time** — a tier opens once you have housed enough residents and produced enough of the previous tier's goods. Coins buy houses; only tiles and time make goods.
- **Market overview** — the market opens on an overview: a production chart of everything your town makes, and sell 25/50/75/all or "keep X, sell the rest" in one go.
- **Idle timers** — putting a building up stays quick, but growing it is the long game. The first upgrade takes 10 minutes on a house or a farm and a full day on an Emporium, and every level after that takes 40% longer than the last, up to a three-day wall.
- **Goals pay gems** — twenty goals, from your first house to your first Emporium, hand out gems rather than pocket change. Only the last few, worth tens of millions, pay coins.
- **Growth costs goods** — a house is worth two residents a level, and upgrading anything now costs a real slice of what your town makes, not pocket change. Farms and quarries cost wood to put up; houses and lumber camps stay coins-only so a brand-new town can always get started.
- **Happiness is a score you can read** — 100 points, and the meter shows every line: where you start, what your townsfolk have and want, parks, workshops beside homes, overcrowding. A need only counts once your town could actually make it, so nobody sulks about bread before you can bake it. Parks are worth up to 20 by the share of residents they cover, and industry costs up to 25 by the residents it sits on top of.
- **One realm** — every mayor builds in the same world. New towns are planted with an empty plot between them, and you can see your neighbours' land and buildings around yours: hover any of it to see whose it is and what they have built.
- **Land changes hands** — an empty plot can go back to the land office for a quarter of its price, or be listed at whatever you like for the mayors whose land touches yours. Buying from a player skips the land-office cooldown entirely, which is the point: when the office makes you wait days, your neighbour does not have to.
- **Growing past a certain size needs the whole chain** — every building, whatever its tier, now wants goods from further up the ladder as it grows: planks from level 5, tools from 9, steel from 13, machines from 17. A farm still reaches level 4 on timber alone, but nothing can be maxed until your town actually makes machines. The upgrade card names the next wall before you hit it.
- **Q and E turn the camera**, alongside WASD to move and right-drag to orbit.
- **Supply chains** — a workshop now wants its materials nearby, and "nearby" is counted in road tiles travelled, not how close the buildings look. A sawmill next door to its lumber camps runs at full speed; one across town crawls. Supply is finite as well: a single camp cannot feed five sawmills, and when workshops compete the closest pairing wins, so a camp on the left feeds the sawmill on the left instead of being spread thin. Nothing ever stops entirely, so goods bought from other mayors are still worth buying, just slower than making them next door. The building card shows exactly how well served each workshop is and what to do about it.
- **Traffic** — the townsfolk on foot are gone. Cars now run between homes and workplaces, and trucks haul between the workshops that actually trade with each other.
- **Fixed** — the happiness meter counted goods your town could not make yet, so a tier-2 town was marked down for having no bread. A need now only counts once you have built something that makes it.
- **Cleaner building card** — opening a building now reads as one short panel: what it makes per hour, two bars for workers and supply, and a single compact upgrade row with its cost and time. Anything you are short of is marked red. Hints are real tooltips that wait for you to hover rather than browser alt-text popping up instantly.
- **Land office, tidied** — the land window now lists only the plots you can actually sell, with the asking price and the office's offer on the same row, and every plot other mayors have put up for sale beneath them. The explainer paragraph is gone.
- **Fixed** — townsfolk asked for bread and tools before the town could make either, so the resource rail showed a permanent deficit for goods you had never produced. A need now only exists once you have built something that makes it.
- **Timers are paced by tier** — the wall a single upgrade can hit now depends on what you are growing. Farms and lumber camps top out at eight hours a level, mills and kilns at twelve, the middle tiers at one and a half days, and only factories and emporiums reach the two- and three-day walls. Early tiers stay brisk while the late game runs for days on its own.
- **Heavy industry costs more to start** — bakeries, smithies, mines, foundries, factories and emporiums roughly doubled in price, so the jump into a new tier is a real decision rather than pocket change. Iron mines dig a little more ore to match.
- **Planks from level 3** — every building now wants planks two levels earlier, and the very last level wants luxuries, so nothing reaches twenty until the town runs the whole chain end to end.
- **Tier gates that actually gate** — the lifetime production a tier demands of the one below it was far too low to matter. Reaching tier 3 now takes about three days of real output, tier 4 a week, tier 5 two weeks and tier 6 six weeks on top of everything before it, so a first Emporium lands near the three-month mark rather than being bought outright.
- **Builders** — every build and every upgrade now occupies one crew for as long as its clock runs. This is what paces the late game: you can no longer set twenty upgrades running at once and wait. Roads still go up instantly and need nobody. Towns start with three crews, and three more can be hired permanently with gems, and when everything is busy the game offers to rush the cheapest job and start what you wanted straight away.
- **Goods climb faster than coins** — resource costs now grow 42% a level against the 35% coins grow, so the last levels of anything are paid for in goods your town has to make rather than money you earned elsewhere.
- **Not everything needs level 20** — parks stop at 12 and warehouses at 16, because there is nothing left to gain past that. Every cap lands where the model finishes a look, and the building card shows the ceiling next to the level.
- **Coins climb gently, goods climb hard** — a maxed Emporium used to cost close to a trillion coins on its own, which turned the whole endgame into a money problem. Coin costs now grow 20% a level instead of 35%, and the goods your town has to make carry the weight instead.
- **The last tier is reachable** — the lifetime production tier 6 asks for was halved, and the day-long walls on tiers 3 and 4 came down to eighteen and twenty-eight hours. A full town now lands around ten weeks with every crew hired, and around four months on the free three.
- **Research** — a fifth window on the dock, and a slow track that runs alongside everything else. Thirty projects in five branches, one at a time, from twelve hours to three days each, paid for in coins and goods. Yield squeezes more out of every workshop, Logistics pushes suppliers further down the road, Construction shortens every timer, Civics houses more people and lifts the mood, and Trade improves what the town hall pays and how much you can store. Finishing the whole board is about two months of always having something in the lab.
- **Terrain** — the ground is no longer the same everywhere. Every tile of every plot is fertile soil, woodland, rock, water or plain grass, worked out from where the plot sits in the realm, so the same square looks the same to everyone. A farm on fertile soil, a lumber camp in woodland and a quarry or mine on rock all produce a quarter more; nothing at all can be built on water, but a park on the bank is worth half as much again in happiness. Press **G** (or the Terrain chip) to tint the ground by type, with the tiles that would pay whatever you are placing ringed in white. A handful of plots are flat grassland with nothing on them and no water either — cheap land, all of it buildable — and you are never founded on one.
- **Land is not all the same** — every plot is made of tiles now: fertile soil, woodland, rock, water and ordinary grass, rolled from the plot's own coordinates so the ground carries across borders. A farm on good soil, a lumber camp in the trees or a quarry on rock all run a quarter harder, water cannot be built on at all, and a rare plot comes up as flat grassland with no bonuses and no obstacles. Buying more land is now a bet on what is under it. Press G, or the Terrain chip, to see what is where; while a building is on the cursor the tiles that would suit it come forward.
- **Two more civic buildings** — a Bathhouse from tier 4 and a Theatre from tier 5, both worth more happiness than a park and both wanting the goods of their tier to put up. Parks are no longer the only way to keep a town cheerful.
- **Every tier feeds the town** — residents now want bricks as well as grain, bread, tools and luxuries, so each tier of the chain either supplies something the townsfolk consume or unlocks a building that makes them happier.
- **Fixed** — a run of numbers the game showed you that it then did not honour. Research shortened nothing (the whole Construction branch was inert), the rates on screen were worked out without your research while the town produced with it, a project that finished while you were away was not banked until you opened the Research window, the income chip and the Mayors board priced a researched town as if it had none, and extra residents from Civics worked in your workshops without counting toward the next tier. Build timers on the cards and progress bars now come from the server, so they always agree with the clock beside them.
- **The market is yours to price** — the ceiling on player offers is gone. The town hall's floor is still the least anyone may ask, because nothing below it could ever be worth taking, but there is no cap above it: what a good is worth to another mayor is between the two of them. Opening a resource now shows how full your storage of it is, since full storage is a stopped production line rather than a full cupboard.
- **Closer neighbours** — new towns are planted with one empty plot between them instead of two, so the land next door is worth buying sooner.
- **Fixed** — three ways to make coins out of nothing, all found before launch. Passing a plot between two accounts at a made-up price and selling it back to the land office minted a quarter of that price each round; the office now only refunds what it was itself paid. Crossing your own offer on the market counted toward lifetime earnings, which pay milestone rewards. And a Trade research bonus on the town hall's price could be arbitraged against the order book's unchanged floor, so that perk is now extra storage instead.
- **Warehouses need people** — a warehouse now wants two residents to open and one more per extension, and holds only what its crew can manage. Extending the shed you have costs fewer residents than putting up another one, so growing beats sprawling.
- **Fixed** — a further run of things found in the last sweep before launch. Two accounts could still wash-trade each other to the sales milestones, so only town-hall sales count toward them now. Two players trading with each other on different goods at the same moment could deadlock, and one of them got an error; every town an order touches is now locked in one fixed order. Escrow coming back from a cancelled order was treated as income and garnished by the bank. Selling a plot to a neighbour walked your own land-office price ladder back down. A plot's price could change while your click was in flight, so the price you saw is now part of the purchase. And a player trading with themselves could set the price every other mayor reads as the market.
- **Dirty industry needs real distance** — a plot is eight tiles across, so every nuisance radius used to fit inside one. Two plots and the problem was solved forever: houses on one, workshops on the other, no penalty at any tier, for good. Heavy industry now reaches further than a plot. A farm is a nuisance to its own street, a bakery costs most of a plot of distance, a foundry more than a whole one, and an emporium nearly two — and the penalty per home climbs with it. Parks reach four tiles instead of three to keep them a real answer.
